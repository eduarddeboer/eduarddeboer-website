# Editorial research — De DDS is niet je due diligence

Status: trial research note  
Date checked: 2026-10-03  
Target: Dutch cornerstone article for eduarddeboer.com

## Central thesis

For an operator exercising full EUDR due diligence, the Due Diligence Statement (DDS) is the declaration submitted after the due-diligence process has led to the conclusion that there is no or only a negligible risk of non-compliance. The DDS is therefore not a substitute for the underlying evidence, analysis, risk assessment or mitigation.

The article should demonstrate this through a practical timber supply-chain case rather than explain the EUDR generically.

## Current legal basis checked

### Article 3

Relevant products may not be placed or made available on the market or exported unless they:
- are deforestation-free;
- were produced in accordance with relevant legislation of the country of production; and
- are covered by a DDS or simplified declaration as required.

Current consolidated text:
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02023R1115-20251226

### Article 4

Article 4(1) requires operators to exercise due diligence before placing relevant products on the market or exporting them, in order to prove compliance with Article 3.

Article 4(2) provides that the DDS is submitted after the operator, on the basis of that due diligence, concludes that the relevant products comply with Article 3. The DDS contains the declaration that due diligence was exercised and no or only negligible risk was found.

Article 4(3) makes the operator responsible for compliance when submitting the DDS.

### Article 8

Due diligence comprises:
1. collection of Article 9 information, data and documents;
2. Article 10 risk assessment;
3. Article 11 risk mitigation where required.

This is the strongest legal support for the article title.

### Article 9

Information must be collected, organised and retained with evidence for each relevant product, including:
- product description and, for wood, species;
- quantity;
- country of production;
- geolocation and production date/time range;
- supplier and customer information;
- adequately conclusive and verifiable information that the product is deforestation-free;
- adequately conclusive and verifiable information concerning legality.

### Article 10

Operators must verify and analyse the Article 9 information and other relevant documentation.

Important criteria for this article:
- Article 10(2)(g): source, reliability, validity and links to other available documentation;
- Article 10(2)(i): complexity of the supply chain and processing stage, especially difficulties connecting relevant products to the plot where the commodity was produced;
- Article 10(2)(j): risk of circumvention or mixing with unknown/non-compliant origin;
- Article 10(2)(m): any information pointing to a risk of non-compliance.

Operators must document the risk assessment and be able to demonstrate how information was checked against the criteria and how the degree of risk was determined.

### Article 11

If the assessment does not reveal no or only negligible risk, risk mitigation is required. Measures may include additional information/documents and independent surveys or audits.

### Article 12

Operators must maintain a due-diligence system and retain all due-diligence documentation for at least five years, making it available to competent authorities on request.

### Annex II

The DDS contains the operator's confirmation that due diligence was carried out and that no or only negligible risk was found.

## Current 2026 Commission interpretation

The Commission's current "Understand Due Diligence" page states that due diligence has three steps: information collection, risk assessment and risk mitigation. It also says due diligence is not a tick-box process and that the DDS/simplified declaration is where the actor confirms the due diligence has been done.

https://green-forum.ec.europa.eu/nature-and-biodiversity/deforestation-regulation-implementation/understand-due-diligence_en

Current 2026 Guidance (Commission Notice C/2026/3896) retains supply-chain complexity as a due-diligence issue. The guidance explains that inconsistency in information and difficulty connecting commodities in the product to plots of production can increase risk.

https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=OJ:C_202603896

Current FAQ page (April 2026 version published 21 August 2026):
https://environment.ec.europa.eu/publications/faq-eudr-implementation_en

## 2026 caveats that must appear

The article is about operators subject to full due diligence under Articles 8–12.

Do not imply:
- that every EUDR actor now carries out full due diligence;
- that every actor submits a DDS;
- that downstream operators/traders still have the pre-2025 obligations;
- that micro or small primary operators use the same declaration route.

Since Regulation (EU) 2025/2650:
- downstream operators are separately defined;
- micro or small primary operators can use a simplified declaration;
- downstream operators and traders have lighter obligations.

The Commission currently states application dates of:
- 30 December 2026 for large and medium operators;
- 30 December 2026 for micro/small operators already covered by EUTR;
- 30 June 2027 for other micro/small operators.

Those dates are not central to this article and should not dominate the opening.

## Case design

Use a fictionalised composite case, explicitly described as such.

Scenario:
- Dutch/EU operator imports 22.4 m³ of sawn timber from a processing/export company outside the EU.
- Evidence pack contains geolocation data, harvesting authorisation, felling records, transport documents, sawmill intake records, packing list, invoice and supplier declaration.
- Every individual document appears plausible.
- The key weakness is the chain between forest/log identifiers, sawmill intake, processed bundles and export consignment.
- The geolocation demonstrates where source material may originate; without the identity bridge it does not by itself demonstrate that this shipment came from those plots.
- Quantity reconciliation is plausible but not demonstrably closed.
- Legality documents may be valid for the concession/year yet still require a demonstrable connection to the material in the shipment.

This case avoids naming a real client or presenting client-specific confidential details.

## Practitioner yield benchmarks supplied by Eduard

These are practical screening benchmarks from Eduard's field experience, not legal EUDR thresholds. They should be used as plausibility checks and, if presented as universal factual claims in public copy, independently sourced or carefully qualified.

### Sawn timber from roundwood
- Maximum practical recovery of A-/export-quality sawn timber: approximately 50–54% of roundwood input.
- Remaining material is not necessarily all waste; lower grades may be sold on the local market, with the rest consisting of sawmill losses, offcuts, residues and other outputs.
- A claimed export-quality recovery substantially above this range should trigger investigation of batch allocation, measurement basis, product mix and possible mixing of production lots.

### Peeled veneer / plywood input
- Approximate absolute maximum peeling recovery for veneer: around 80%.
- In the Cambodian factory previously audited by Eduard, approximately 70% was achieved and regarded as a good result.
- This benchmark is especially relevant for future composite-product, veneer and plywood input-output reconciliation examples.

## Desired analytical movement

1. Start with a dossier that looks complete.
2. Explain what the DDS legally is.
3. Work backwards through the chain.
4. Show that document presence is not the same as evidential linkage.
5. Test identity, origin, time, quantity, legality/deforestation-free evidence and residual risk.
6. Connect the analysis to Articles 9 and 10.
7. End with one practical backwards-tracing test.

## Desired reader reaction

Not:
> I now understand the EUDR.

But:
> We have those documents. Can we actually prove that they belong to the same material flow?
